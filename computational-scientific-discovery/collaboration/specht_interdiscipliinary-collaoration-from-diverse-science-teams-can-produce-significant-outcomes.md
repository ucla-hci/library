### Main finding
> ... team diversity has a positive impact on scientific output (i.e., the number of journal papers and citations) through the mediation of the interdisciplinarity of the collaborative process, as evidenced by publishing in and citing more diverse sources.

### Relevance to informal peer support in research group meetings

**Useful, but indirect.** The study was not designed to study informal peer support and did not operationalize or measure it. Its quantitative model concerns team composition, bibliometric interdisciplinarity, and group outcomes. However, its open-ended responses identify several interpersonal practices that overlap strongly with informal peer support:

- listening to colleagues with different expertise and learning from them;
- empathy, friendliness, responsiveness, mutual trust, and respect;
- “supporting everyone on the team” and encouraging openness to different voices and ideas;
- fluid leadership—members leading when needed and stepping back to let others lead;
- senior researchers advising while postdocs, PhD students, and early-career researchers contribute labor and new ideas;
- keeping technical and conceptual subgroups included in whole-group discussions rather than allowing expertise-based silos;
- helping colleagues negotiate terminology barriers, disciplinary discomfort, and different ways of working.

Face-to-face meetings were described as pivotal for attention, accountability, coordination, and getting work done. Momentum and participation often declined between meetings, and a small core of “doers” frequently carried the work. This makes the paper particularly useful for asking *when and where* peer support occurs: meetings may temporarily concentrate supportive interactions that are harder to sustain asynchronously.

The paper suggests a plausible mechanism for my research: **diversity creates both learning opportunities and integration costs; informal peer support during meetings may help teams convert those differences into shared understanding and coordinated work.** Candidate observable indicators include listening and acknowledgment, translation across expertise, invitations to speak, encouragement, practical help, advice, empathy, shared leadership, and efforts to reconnect subgroups.

This interpretation should remain tentative. The interpersonal findings came from voluntary open-ended survey comments, were not systematically measured, and cannot show that peer support caused productivity or satisfaction. The sample was also small (22 voluntarily assembled environmental-science working groups at three synthesis centers), and these groups met repeatedly with institutional support for as long as four years, so they differ from ordinary lab or research-group meetings.
