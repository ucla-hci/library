## Key Points

### The origin of abduction by Peirce
> ... there occurs in science and in veryday life a distinctive pattern of reasoning wherein explanatory hypotheses are formed and accepted. He called this kind of reasoning "abduction."

### Formal definition of abduction
```
D is a collection of data (facts, observations, givens).
H explains D (would, if true, explain D).
No other hypothesis can explain D as well as H does.-
Therefore, H is probably true.
```

### The scope of abduction
Goes beyond just generating hypotheses but also validating them
> ... we use the term for the whole process of generation, criticism, and acceptance of explanatory hypotheses

### What count as a good abduction (inference to the best explanation)
- explains the symptoms
- be plausible
- be significantly better than alternative explanations

The formalized metrics:
> - how decisively *H* surpasses the alternatives
> - how good *H* is by itself, independently of considering the alternatives ...
> - judgments of the reliability of the data
> - how much confidence there is that all plausible explanations have been considered ... ?

### A causal view of abduction
> An explanation is an assignment of causal responsibility; it tells a causal story ... Thus, finding possible explanations is finding possible causes of the thing to be explained, and so abduction is essentially a process of reasoning from effect to cause.

### Relation to Theory of Mind
> ... when we infer from a person's behavior to some fact about her mental state, we are inferring that the fact explains the behavior better than some other competing explanation does.

### How to rule out alternatives (to infer the best explanation)
- A cause can be ruled out if it would have resulted in "symptoms" that did not actually appear
- A cause can be ruled out if it would not result in some important observed "symptoms"

### On inference
> Inferences are movements of thought within the sphere of belief.

### Deduction vs. abduction vs. induction
> Deductions are truth *preserving*, where as successful abductions may be said to be *truth producing*.
> ... Abduction can thus make the leap from "observation language" to "theory language."

> ... inductive generalizations can be insightfully analyzed as special cases of abductions.

### A leap exists in making abductions
> We should (and do) make the inferential leap when we judge that "no other hypothesis can explain *D* as well as *H* deos."

### How explanations are subjective
> The things that will satisfy us as accounting for *f* will depend on why we are trying to account for *f* ...

### Control experiments are also abductions
> ... the whole notion of a "controlled experiment" is covertly based on abduction. What is being "controlled for" is always an alternative way of explaining the outcome.

### Explanation vs. prediction
> Predictions, then, are neither abductions nor (typically) deductions. This is contrary, both to the view that predictions are deductions and to Harman's view that all nondeductive inferences are abductions. Rather, predictions and abductions are two distinct kinds of plausible inference. Abductions go from data to explanatory hypothesis; predictions go from hypothesis to expected data.
