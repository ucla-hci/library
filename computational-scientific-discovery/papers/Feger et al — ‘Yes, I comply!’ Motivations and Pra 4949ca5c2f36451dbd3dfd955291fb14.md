# Feger et al. —  ‘Yes, I comply!’: Motivations and Practices around Research Data Management and Reuse across Scientific Fields

```
@article{feger2020yes,
intro={As science becomes increasingly data-intensive, the requirements for comprehensive Research Data Management (RDM) grow. This often overwhelms scientists, requiring more workload and training. The failure to conduct effective RDM leads to producing research artefacts that cannot be reproduced or reused. Past research placed high value on supporting data science workers but focused mainly on data production, collection, processing, and sensemaking. In order to understand practices and needs of data science workers in relation to documentation, preservation, sharing, and reuse, we conducted a cross-domain study with 15 scientists and data managers from diverse scientific domains. We identified five core concepts which describe requirements, drivers, and boundaries in the development of commitment for RDM, essential for generating reproducible research artefacts: Practice, Adoption, Barriers, Education, and Impact. Based on those concepts, we introduce a stage-based model of personal RDM commitment evolution. The model can be used to drive the design of future systems that support a transition to open science. We discuss infrastructure, policies, and motivations involved at the stages and transitions in the model. Our work supports designers in understanding the constraints and challenges involved in designing for reproducibility in an age of data-driven science.},
```

# One Sentence



The authors introduce a stage-based model of personal RDM commitment evolution based on the five core concepts which describe requirements, drivers, and boundaries in the development of commitment for RDM, essential for generating reproducible research artifacts: Practice, Adoption, Barriers, Education, and Impact. 

# More Sentences



**Practice** reflects the diversity in terms of data collection, resource sharing, and reuse within and across diverse fields of science. Related to this core concept, participants stressed the role of data management tools and services, with particular regard to the exchange of information within and across institutes.

**Education** has shown to be a key barrier for effective RDM. The research data managers highlighted causes and consequences of missing training in the scientific community. They stressed that RDM needs to become an essential part in researchers’ formal training and further highlighted upcoming challenges around reuse in computational and big data science as well as a lack of established and widely used citation standards in computational research.

**Adoption** of RDM practices that is often driven by institutional policies and submission rules. Additional drivers of commitment include the desire for increased visibility, positive encouragement by team members and supervisors, and frustration about the status quo. However, the participants stressed that any effort needs to be met by a suitable technical infrastructure. This infrastructure, often provided or recommended by institutes must address various technical barriers for RDM.

In the **Barriers** core concept, four technical key barriers are described: interoperability of data types, forms, and formats; dealing with closed and proprietary tools; handling large data volumes; and facing computational challenges that become increasingly relevant in data-intensive science.
**Impact** involved that participants provided strong examples of how effective RDM and openness in science benefits researchers and institutes. Here, transparency becomes key in negotiating with funding agencies and allows to demonstrate responsible use of unique data sources.

# Key Points



The model improves our understanding of how researchers transition from non-reproducible practices to sustained commitment for comprehensive RDM.
