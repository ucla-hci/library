# Schunn & Klahr — A 4-space model of scientific discovery

```
Schunn, C.D. and Klahr, D., 1995. A 4-space model of scientific discovery. In Proceedings of the 17th annual conference of the cognitive science society (pp. 106-111). Mahwah, NJ: Lawrence Erlbaum Associates.
```

# One Sentence


This paper presents a model of scientific discovery that consists of 

- data representation (identifying features worth studying in the data),
- hypothesis formulation (proposing causal relationship between features and phenomena of interest),
- experimental paradigms (selecting a class of experiments for testing the hypothesis)
- experiment (instantiating a specific experimental design from the chosen paradigm)

### Three ways of identifying features in the data representation space

- Notice invariants: notice something that never changes and try to “perturb” it
- Analogy: analogize the data to something else, e.g., this protein works just like that other protein
- Brute-force search: exhaustively examine all known aspects given the data

### Narrowly overfitting to data analyses?
Similar to the Simon's school of scientific discovery, this model assumes that the discovery process starts with and operates within some data and the goal is to identify which of the data's attributes exhibit certain relationship with the "phenomena of interest".

**Both, depending on the intended scope.** It is an elegant and parsimonious model of an *inquiry episode after a problem and domain have been fixed*. It is also broader than a model of analyzing a fixed dataset: experiments produce new data, while search in the data-representation space can change which objects and features the scientist sees in those data. The four spaces neatly distinguish finding regularities, explaining them, choosing a kind of intervention, and instantiating that intervention.

It is too narrow, however, as a model of scientific discovery *tout court*. The MilkTruck task that motivated it supplies the phenomenon, research goal, available operations, and criterion of success. The model therefore brackets the selection and construction of research questions; major changes in ontologies or theoretical frameworks; the invention of instruments and measurement systems; non-empirical work such as mathematical reasoning; and the social processes of criticism, replication, and consensus formation. Some of these activities could be squeezed into one of the four spaces, but doing so would classify them rather than explain them.

The most charitable reading, then, is as a **meso-level model of empirical hypothesis-testing**, not a complete theory of science. At that level its abstraction is a strength. A more general account would at least add an upstream question/problem space and make the relations among spaces explicitly recursive, since theories affect what counts as data and experiments generate the data that can force representational and theoretical change.
