## Historical Contexts

### What does “scientific discovery” mean?

> In the broadest sense, “discovery” is a synonym for “successful scientific endeavor”
> 

### The historical, two-part theory of scientific discovery

Formally, it’s called “the distinction between the context of discovery. and the context of justification”

> … the generation of new knowledge came to be clearly and explicitly distinguished from its assessment, …
> 

The part of discovering new ideas and knowledge is distinct from the part of assessing and justifying such discovery, as elaborated in the following points

> … neither is it possible to prescribe a logical method that produces new ideas nor is it possible to reconstruct logically the process of discovery. Only the process of testing is amenable to logical investigation.
> 

> … the generation of a new idea is an intuitive, nonrational process; it cannot be subject to normative analysis … can only be the subject of psychology, sociology, and other empirical sciences.
> 

> … the procedures and methods for arriving at new hypotheses and ideas are no guarantee that the hypothesis or idea that is thus formed is necessarily the best or the correct one.
> 

Others do propose an alternative view that considers the “non-logical”, “eureka” process is only a part of discovery:

> … to conceptualize the generation of new knowledge as an extended process that includes a creative act as well as some process of articulating and developing the creative idea.
>

In the ancient Greek context, analytic methods in mathematics, geometry, and philosophy were not clearly separated; the notion of finding or discovering things by analysis was relevant in all these fields.

## Discovery as Abduction

Proposed by (Hanson 1958), the abductive process SD works as follows

> 1. Some surprising, astonishing phenomena *p1, p2, p3 …* are encountered
2. But *p1, p2, p3* … would not be surprising were an hypothesis of *H*’s type to obtain. They would follow as a matter of course from something like *H* and would be explained by it.
3. Therefore there is good reason for elaborating an hypothesis of type H—for proposing it as a possible hypothesis from whose assumption *p1, p2, p3* … might be explained
> 

### Limitations of the abductive view of SD

1. It is not really a logical process

> … this process is a “logical” process only in the wildest sense whereby the term “logical” is understood as synonymous with “rational”.
> 
1. Unclear how to choose an H from multiple hypotheses

> … too permissive … There will be several hypotheses that are explanations for phenomena *p1, p2, p3 …*
> 
1. Unclear how to find hypotheses H to choose from

> … does not explain the very act of conceiving a hypothesis or hypothesis-type
> 

### Selective vs. creative abduction

Selective abduction “involves selecting a hypothesis from a set of known hypotheses” such as medical diagnosis of identifying what makes a patient sick from a set of known diseases;

Creative abduction “involves generating a new, plausible hypothesis” such as medical research that detects the existence of and defines a new disease.

Likely there is no strict difference between these two abduction but rather a “gradual transition”.

## Discovery as Problem Solving by Heuristic Search

The fundamental assumption is treating SD as “a form of problem-solving activity” where a problem solution is “a sequence of operations leading from the initial state to the goal state”:

> The aim of constructing a logic of discovery thus becomes the aim of constructing a heuristics for the efficient search for solutions to problems.
> 

### The nature of heuristic search

- Unlike algorithms that guarantee a solution, heuristic search  “lead to results that are merely provisional and plausible” (i.e., not guaranteed to be the best, or to be even there?);
- Heuristic search is “advantageous because they are more efficient than exhaustive random trial and error searches”
- Heuristic search is considered “a normative theory of discovery”

### Example strategies of heuristic search

- Means-ends analysis “involves identifying specific differences between the present and the goal situation and searches for operators (processes that will change the situation) that are associated with the differences”;
- Divide-and-conquer “divide(s) the problem into sub-problem” and “begin(s) solving the one with the smallest number of unknowns to be determined”

### Limitations of the heuristic search view of SD

- For SD, often the problem space is complex, ill-defined, or undefined to be readily available for a heuristic search
- The same goes for the solution space and the goal state, which can both be ill or undefined

## Cognitive Perspectives on Discovery

### Discovery as a creative process

Boden’s definition of creativity

> … psychological creativity (P-creativity) and historical creativity (H-creativity). P-creativity is a development that is new, surprising and important to the particular person who comes up with it. H-creativity , by contrast, is radically novel, surprising, and important—it is generated for the first time.
> 

### Analogy enables creative discovery

**Positive vs. negative analogy**:

- Positive analogy: aspects that are similar between the source and target domains, e.g., the randomness of motion of gas molecules is similar to that of billiard balls;
- Negative analogy: aspects that are dissimilar between the source and target domains, e.g., the sizes and appearances are different between gas molecules and billiard balls.
- Neutral analogy refers to cases where it remains unclear whether the analogy is positive or negative.

**Horizontal vs. vertical analogy**:

- Horizontal analogy compares aspects between the source and target domains, e.g., how sound echoes is analogous to light reflects and sound’s loudness is analogous to light’s brightness
- Vertical analogy compares relationships between the source and target domains, e.g., the  relationship of sounds’ physical properties causing loudness is analogous to lights’ physical properties causing brightness

**Factors that influence analogical thinking:**

Based on the multi-constraint theory of analogical reasoning (for scientific domains or beyond)

1. direct similarity between the elements involved
2. the structural parallels (how elements are structured?)
3. the purpose of drawing the analogy

### Mental modeling enables creative discovery

> … much of human reasoning … takes place through mental modeling … In model-based reasoning, the mind constructs a structural representation of a real-world or imaginary situation and manipulate this structure.
> 

Types of mental modeling include:

- analogical modeling
- visual modeling
- simulative modeling

### Understanding philosophy’s approach to understand SD

> … philosophy is a normative project; it determines norms for scientific practice. … only the justification of ideas, not their generation, can be the subject of philosophical (normative) analysis. Discovery, by contrast, can only be a topic for empirical study.

### Kuhn’s view of discovery as anomaly detection

> The process of discovery involves several aspects: observations of an anomalous phenomenon, attempts to conceptualize it, and changes in the paradigm so that the anomaly can be accommodated.
> 

Kuhn has a similar view on novelty too

> … novelty ordinarily emerges only for the man who, knowing with precision what he should expect, is able to recognize that something has gone wrong.
> 

### Consequential vs. generative justification

**Consequential justification.** The traditional two-part theory considers consequential justification, which means that discovery needs to be able to successfully predict something new (i.e., not yet known).

> … “consequential justification,” the notion that a hypothesis is established if successful *novel* predictions are derived from the theory or claim
> 

An example might be drug discovery’s hit ID and lead optimization phases that aim to discovery new ligands to inhibit proteins complicit in certain diseases’ process.

**Generative justification**.

> … justifies a knowledge claim by deriving it from results that are already established.
> 

An example might be drug discovery’s target ID given some known ligands already working (but the precise target and the associated protein network remain unknown).

### Preliminary appraisal

Two specific approaches:

1. Use heuristic criteria to “weakly” evaluate a hypothesis’ worthiness for further stronger evaluation

> … articulates criteria for the evaluation of a hypothesis prior to rigorous derivation or testing. It aids the decision about whether to take that hypothesis seriously enough to develop it further and test it.
> 
1. Using some kinds of structure of framework within which to test a hypothesis

> … reasoning processes governed by reasoning strategies … (such as) The discovery of the mechanism of protein synthesis involved the instantiation of an abstract schema for chemical reactions: reactant1 + reactant2 = product
