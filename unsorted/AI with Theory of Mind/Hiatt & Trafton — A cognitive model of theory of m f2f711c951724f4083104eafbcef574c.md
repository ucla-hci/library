# More Sentences


> The model uses a selection process to identify the beliefs and knowledge others may have; then, to predict the desires and behaviors of others, it uses the identified concepts as input to its own decision-making mechanisms, simulating what the model would do in the other’s place
> 

# Key Points


### The three views of how ToM works

1. **Theory-theory**: “… children learn a set of causal laws, or theories, about the beliefs and desires of people in general …”
2. **Simulation theory**:
    1. “representing the mental states of others …”
    2. “using their own decision-making systems to operate on these foreign mental states to predict others’ behavior”
3. **Selection from multiple beliefs**:
    1. “generate and represent multiple possible beliefs”
    2. “a selection process (SP) that uses inhibition to reason about others’ beliefs”

# Other Notes


### Background about the ACT-R architecture

The ACT-R (Adaptive Control of Thought-Rational) architecture is a model of human cognition. It’s designed to simulate how people think and learn. Think of it like a computer program that tries to mimic the way our brains work. It breaks down human thought processes into basic units called "modules," which handle things like memory, perception, and decision-making. These modules work together to process information and make decisions, just like how different parts of your brain communicate to help you understand the world and react to it.

The basic units in ACT-R are called "modules," and each module is responsible for a specific type of cognitive function. Here’s a breakdown of some key modules:

1. **Declarative Memory Module**: This is like a mental database where facts and information are stored. For example, your knowledge that Paris is the capital of France is stored here.
2. **Procedural Memory Module**: This handles the "how-to" knowledge, like how to ride a bike or tie your shoes. It’s where skills and habits are stored.
3. **Goal Module**: This keeps track of what you’re trying to achieve at any moment. If you’re cooking dinner, your goal might be to make sure everything is ready at the same time.
4. **Perceptual-Motor Modules**: These are responsible for processing what you see and hear (perception) and controlling your physical actions (motor). For example, when you see a green light, these modules help you decide to step on the gas pedal.
5. **Imaginal Module**: This helps with visualizing and manipulating information in your mind, like when you’re mentally rotating an object or planning your next move in a game.

Each of these modules operates semi-independently but communicates with the others to help you think, make decisions, and act. ACT-R tries to model how these different parts of your brain work together to handle everyday tasks.
