# One Sentence


This paper proposes a  theory of scientific discovery that unified two traditionally disjoint views of concept-formation vs. problem-solving as a dual-space search process: searching in the “space of rules” for a concept or solution, which is then tested on a subset from the “space of instances”.

# Key Points


### The general, historical view of SD

> … must master two related skills: knowing where to look and understanding what is seen. The first skill—*experimental design*—involves the design of experimental and observational procedures. The second skill—*hypothesis formation—*involves the formation and evaluation of theory.
> 

### The two disjoint views of SD

> … *concept-formation view*. The argument here is that much of scientific reasoning consists of forming new concepts on the basis of experimental evidence. … *problem-solving view*, is exemplified by … a search process similar in structure to any problem-solving task, albeit within a complex search space.
> 

### The unified view of SD

> Simon and Lea’s (1974) insight that both concept learning and problem solving are information-gathering tasks and that both employ guided search processes.
> 

### The importance of having both hypothesis and experiment spaces

> … hypotheses can be generated either from prior knowledge or by generating from the results of prior experiments.
> 

> None of hour subjects started with the correct general frame. However, once they were driven to it by earlier failed hypotheses and observation of results, they were able to form the correct hypothesis.
> 

### When/who to use which: hypothesis and experiment spaces

> Subjects who can construct the correct frame from information in long-term memory are Theorists. Those who are unable to construct the correct frame from information in long-term memory are Experimenters.
> 

> Experts tend to search the hypothesis space, and novices tend to search the experiment space.
> 

# Other Notes


### Confirmation bias in designing experiments

> … when subjects design experiments, they show a pervasive confirmation bias … They propose a single hypothesis and seek evidence that will confirm, rather than disconfirm, it. … scientists and nonscientists did not differ in this regard.
> 

### Confirmation bias and positive test strategy

> … “confirmation bias” … prefer to select instances that they. expect to confirm rather than disconfirm their hypothesis.
> 

> … most people follow a heuristic they call a “positive test strategy”—”a tendency to examine cases hypothesized to be targets” … (when) the probability of confirming one’s hypothesis is high, then positive tests will not provide any useful information.
> 

> … a positive test strategy may be a useful heuristic in the early stage of investigation, as it allows the subject to determine those types of instances that are worthy of further experimentation.
>
