### Historical origins of HCI rooted in cognitive psychology
> In the early 1980s, optimism abounded as to how the field of cognitive psychology could contribute to the development of HCI.

But now we seem to have abandoned the cognitive aspects ...

### What a theoretical approach should not be
> A core lesson learned, however, is that you cannot simply lift theories out of an established field (viz., cognitive psychology)---theories that have been developed to explain specific phenomena about cognition--and then use them to explain other kinds of seemingly related phenomena in a different domain.

### How cognitive theory was first applied in HCI
> - Informative (providing useful research findings)
> - Predictive (providing tools to model user behavior)
> - Prescriptive (providing advice as to how to design or evaluate)

- **Informative:** Findings from cognitive psychology were imported as facts about users. Rogers uses Miller's research on short-term-memory capacity—the “7 ± 2” result—as an example; in HCI it was often simplified into a rule about how many items an interface or menu should contain, even though that was not what Miller studied.
- **Predictive:** Card, Moran, and Newell's Model Human Processor and GOMS family modeled expert interaction as goals, operators, methods, and selection rules in order to predict task-completion time. In Project Ernestine, a CPM-GOMS analysis correctly predicted that a proposed telephone-operator workstation would be slower than the existing one, contrary to the designers' expectations.
- **Prescriptive:** The cognitive walkthrough translated a theory of exploratory learning into an evaluation method: an evaluator steps through a task and asks whether a new user will form the right goal, notice the right control, connect it to the desired result, and understand the system's feedback.

### Reviewing Theory for HCI: the Ecological Approach (most notably--affordance)
Below is a winded way of saying an approach that produced "affordance"-like concepts:
> The ecological approach evolved primarily from Gibson's view that psychology should be the study of the interaction between the human and its environment. This approach aims to provide a carefully detailed description of the environment and people's ordinary activities within it.
> A principal focus in the original ecological framework was to analyze invariant structures in the environment in relation to human perception and action.

Here comes "affordance":
> Ecological constraints refer to structures in the external world that guide people's actions rather than those that are determined by internal cognitive processes.
> ... *affordance*, in the context of HCI, has been used to refer to attributes of objects that allow people to know how to use them, to afford is "to give a clue" (Norman, 1988).

Lessons learned from affordance as a theoretical approach of HCI:
> ... a problem of appropriate the concept of affordance in this manner is that it puts the onus on designers to use their intuition to decide what are affordable objects at the interface. No abstractions, methods, rules, or guidelines are available to help--only analogies drawn from the real world. The lack of guidance has unfortunately led to the concept being somewhat glibly used.

> The downside of the concept of affordance being popularized in this way is that the richness and contextual background of the original theory have been lost, so that its significance is perceived only at a superficial level.

> ... the role of theory here is largely descriptive

### Why CogSci for HCI failed
> ... why basic cognitive theories failed to make a substantial contribution to HCI was the mismatch between the cognitive framework (information processing model) and the phenomena of interest (human-computer interaction). The former had been developed to explain human cognition in terms of hypothetical processes exclusively inside the mind of an individual. The latter is essentially about how people interact with external representations at the computer interface. 

> ... what was problematic with the classical cognitive science approach was not the conceptual framework per se, but its exclusive focus on modeling the cognitive processes that occurred within one individual.

### Reviewing Theory for HCI: External Cognition
> ... the interaction between internal and external representations when performing cognitive tasks (e.g., learning)

Why the author believes that this theory worked for HCI
> ... the way theory has been used to inform the cognitive dimensions and design approaches, respectively, is largely *generative*.

### Reviewing Theory for HCI: Distributed Cognition
The expected outcome:
> ... an explication of the complex interdependencies between people and artifacts in their work activities.

Compared with Activity Theory:
> ... no set of interlinked concepts exists that can be readily used to extract insights from the data ... the distributed cognition approach is much harder to apply because it lacks a set of explicit features to look for: nor is there a check list or recipe that can be easily followed when conducting the analysis

> ... the outcome of doing an analysis using AT concepts is at a higher level and does not map readily onto the level required for contemplating design solutions.

### Activity theory as one example of theory informing HCI
> Activity Theory, originating from Soviet psychology ... It was regarded as a unifying theoretical framework for HCI, able to provide the rigor of the scientific method of traditional cognitive science while taking into account social and contextual aspects ...

Activity Theory takes purposeful, socially situated activity—not an isolated user or a decontextualized mental process—as its unit of analysis. A person or group acts on an object to produce an outcome through mediating artifacts, including computers; that activity is also shaped by its community, rules, division of labor, and history. For HCI, this makes an interface one tool within a larger practice and provides concepts for analyzing how individual actions, social organization, and technology influence one another over time.
