### Empathy building is currently lacking in AI's support for UX practices
The authors argue that empathy building is not merely an output of UX work; it is a cognitive process that happens while practitioners listen to users, interpret ambiguous evidence, and synthesize what they have learned. UX methods therefore function as mindsets and scaffolds for understanding users, not just as procedures for producing artifacts. An AI system can generate a plausible persona, code interview transcripts, or summarize reviews while bypassing precisely the engagement through which a practitioner develops that understanding.

The review finds this mismatch in both the *discover* and *define* phases. In discover work, research often treats personas as deliverables that can be generated from data, although constructing a persona is itself a synthesis activity through which designers learn about users. Synthetic user information can also collapse people into statistically likely profiles and reinforce stereotypes. In define work, fully automating qualitative coding can remove researchers from codebook construction—the interpretive stage where they form an emotional connection to the data, notice surprises, debate ambiguities, and bring their own perspectives to bear. Studies reviewed by the authors found that simplistic automation could disrupt established workflows and reduce efficiency or quality rather than improve them.

The paper consequently distinguishes between automating an artifact and supporting the learning that produces it. It suggests lower AI delegability for interpretive work such as building a codebook, but greater delegability for mechanical work such as applying an established codebook to the remaining data. Interactive systems such as Cody and PaTAT are presented as better models: they automate repetitive operations while keeping practitioners in control of decisions and leaving room for reflection and learning.

The design implication is that AI assistance for UX should be evaluated not only by speed or output quality, but also by whether practitioners encounter the evidence, retain agency, revise their interpretations, and leave with a richer understanding of users. Useful AI might surface contrasting evidence, preserve links from summaries or personas to source data, expose uncertainty, invite comparison and disagreement, and automate repetitive work only after the empathy-building judgments have been made. In the authors' framing, the goal is to support the UX mindset rather than simply automate UX methods.
