## Key Points
<!-- the most important things in this paper -->

### Key findings corresponding to the three hypotheses

1. **H1: People will rate a design lower when they see it alongside alternatives than when they see it alone.** Supported. All three designs received lower mean ratings in the multiple-design condition. The difference was statistically significant for the Circular and Linear designs, but not for the Tabular design (which approached significance).
2. **H2: People who see alternatives will feel less pressure to be positive and will make fewer positive comments.** Supported. Participants made significantly fewer positive comments about every design when it appeared among three alternatives. They also made significantly more negative comments about the weakest design, suggesting that alternatives made criticism easier.
3. **H3: People who see alternatives will offer more suggestions for improvement.** Not supported. Showing multiple designs did not produce significantly more substantial suggestions. Usability testing helped users identify problems and compare alternatives, but did not by itself turn them into designers capable of proposing solutions.

## Take-Away
<!-- critiques, ideas, actionable things, etc. -->

### Designers should also explore multiple alternatives

This study supports a narrower claim: **designers should put multiple alternatives in front of users during usability testing**. Doing so reduced inflated ratings, elicited more candid criticism, and sometimes enabled users to reject a design outright. The authors therefore argue that low-cost prototypes let teams continue exploring alternatives beyond initial ideation, helping them choose the right design before refining it.

However, the experiment did **not** compare designers who generated one concept with designers who generated several, so it does not directly prove that exploring multiple alternatives improves designers' own process or final design quality. For that stronger claim, see Dow et al., *Parallel Prototyping Leads to Better Design Results, More Divergence, and Increased Self-Efficacy* (2010), which experimentally compared parallel and serial prototyping and found better design outcomes from parallel exploration.
