# Klahr & Dunbar: Dual Space Search During Scientific Reasoning (SDDS)

## One Sentence
The 1988 cognitive science paper introducing the Scientific Discovery as Dual Search (SDDS) model, characterizing scientific reasoning as search in two interacting spaces — a hypothesis space and an experiment space — with three core processes: search, test, and evaluate.

## More Sentences
Klahr and Dunbar conducted controlled experiments in which subjects were asked to discover how an unencountered function of an electronic device worked, producing two archetypal strategies: "Theorists" who searched the hypothesis space first, and "Experimenters" who drove data collection from the experiment space. SDDS identifies three core scientific processes: searching for a candidate hypothesis, designing experiments to test it, and evaluating evidence against the hypothesis. The model has become foundational for understanding both human scientific reasoning and how to design computational support for it.

## Key Points

### Dual-Space Architecture
> "Scientific reasoning requires search in two distinct but related search spaces: hypothesis space and experiment space."

### Hypothesis Evaluation as a Distinct Process
SDDS explicitly separates "evaluate evidence" from "search hypothesis space" and "design experiment" — underscoring that hypothesis validation is a cognitive sub-task requiring dedicated support.

### Theorist vs. Experimenter Strategies
Two discovery strategies with different entry points into the dual space have direct implications for how HCI tools should scaffold the hypothesis-validation phase differently for different user types.

## Other Notes
Authors: David Klahr, Kevin Dunbar. Published in Cognitive Science, Vol. 12, No. 1 (1988). Note: this is the primary theoretical anchor for the research context — not an HCI paper, but cited as foundational in HCI work on scientific discovery tools. Over 1,100 citations. ## Take-Away
The theoretical foundation for the research program: any HCI paper on hypothesis validation in scientific contexts should be read against SDDS, which specifies that hypothesis evaluation is a distinct process that can be supported (or hindered) by tool design.
