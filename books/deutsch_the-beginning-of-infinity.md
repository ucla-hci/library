## Key Points

### The importance of fallibilism

> ... the recognition that there are no authoritative sources of knowledge, nor any reliable means of justifying ideas as being true or probable -- is called *fallibilism*

> Fallibilists expect even their best and most fundamental explanations to contain misconceptions in addition to truth, and so they are predisposed to try to change them for the better.

> So it is fallibilism, not mere rejection of authority, that is essential for the initiation of unlimited knowledge growth -- the beginning of infinity.

**My take**: the growth comes from a committed acknowledgement that "you can trust no one"---no matter how many sources of justifications there are, there is always a possibility to prove something wrong.

### The relationship between theory and data
> We never know any data before interpreting it through theories. All observations are, as Popper put it, *theory-laden*, and hence fallible, as all our theories are.

**My take**: Data as we see (interpret) it could be wrong (misinterpreted) because the theory that governs our interpretation could be wrong.

### The essence of experimental testing
> ... is that there are at least two apparently viable theories known about the issue in question, making conflicting predictions that can be distinguished by the experiment.

### Conflicting ideas is the driver of scientific inquiry, and how to define the term "problem"
> ... conflicting ideas in a broader sense are the occasion for all rational thought and inquiry. For example, if we are simply curious about something, it means that we believe that our existing ideas do not adequately capture or explain it. So, we have some *criterion* that our best existing explanation fails to meet. The criterion and the existing explanation are conflicting ideas. I shall call a situation in which we experience conflicting ideas a *problem*.
