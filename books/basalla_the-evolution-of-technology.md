## Key Points
<!-- the most important things in this paper -->

### Echoing Buxton's "long nose of innovation"
> The existence of continuity implies that novel artifacts can only arise from antecedent artifacts--that new kinds of made things are never pure creations of theory, ingenuity, or fancy.

> If technology is to evolve, then novelty must appear in the midst of the continuous. ... The conclusion drawn from this survey is that any society, at any time, commands more potential for technological innovation than it can ever hope to exploit.

### How necessity is culturally-dependent
(Necessity as the one that is thought to drive technological evolution)
> ... necessity is not something imposed by nature upon humanity but is a conceptual category created by cultural choice.

### How technology is superfluous
The following two viewpoints from two different philosophers take it one step further to refute the idea that technology is "destined" to be invented because there is a need for it.

> Because technology is not necessary in meeting the animal needs of humans, philosopher José Ortega y Gasset defines technology as the production of the superfluous.

> According to French philosopher Gaston Bachelard the conquest of the superfluous gives us a greater spiritual stimulus than the conquest of the necessary because humans are creations of desire, not need.

> Agriculture and cooking are not prerequisites for human survival; they only become necessary when we choose to define our well-being as including them.

### Non-fundamental needs give rise to diverse technologies
> ... the history of technology is a part of the much broader history of human aspirations, and the plethora of made things are a product of human minds replete with fantasies, longings, wants, and desires. The artifactual world would exhibit far less diversity if it operated primarily under the constraints imposed by fundamental needs.

## Other Notes
<!-- other things, not so important, but good to know -->

### Indication of the scale of diversity
> In the United States alone more than 4.7 million patents have been issued since 1790. If each of these patents is counted as the equivalent of an organic species, then the technological can be said to have a diversity three times greater than the organic.

> In 1867, ... five hundred different kinds of hammers were produced in Birmingham, England.

### How tool use is unique to humans
> Even the earliest and crudest tools produced by humans imply considerable foresight and a level of mentality that sets them apart from the most sophisticated tools made by animals. As Karl Marx pointed out, the worst human architect is superior to the best insect nest or hive builder because only humans are able to envision structures in their imagination before erecting them.

### One of the ancestors of symbiotic computing
> Butler's influence is also evident in modern speculative essays that predict either the coming of a new symbiotic relationship between humans and machines or the supersession of humankind by new forms of technology that are capable of self-replication, such as robots and computers.

### Take-away from Pitt-Rivers' work
> ... every made thing could be placed within a sequence, which itself was interconnected to other sequences, and that, if we followed these backward in time, they would converge, leading us to the earliest human artifacts.

### Marx's view on technological evolution
> Invention is a social process, argued Marx, that rests on the accumulation of many minor improvements, not the heroic efforts of a few geniuses.

### Related to Thagard's theory that creativity is combinatorial
> Ogburn ... defining invention as combining existing and known elements of culture in order to form a new element.
